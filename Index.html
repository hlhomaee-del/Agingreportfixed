Aging Reports

An insurance aging report organizes outstanding payer balances by how long they have been unpaid. It helps the biller identify claims outstanding from third-party payers and decide which claims need follow-up first.

Inspection Activity: What Can You Find on This Aging Report?

Check every item you can actually find in the report above. Some choices sound related to billing, but they do not belong on an aging report.

Aging Report

Shows unpaid balances organized by how long they have been outstanding. It helps identify claims that need follow-up.

Payment Report

Shows payments received and posted, such as check/EFT amounts, payment dates, and payer remittance information.

Appointment Report

Shows scheduled visits, arrival status, missed appointments, cancellations, and provider schedule information.

Patient Ledger

Shows one patient’s account history: charges, payments, adjustments, and current balance over time.

EOB

Explains how a payer processed one claim: allowed amount, paid amount, denial, adjustment, and patient responsibility.

A/P Report

Shows money the practice owes to outside parties, such as vendors, rent, equipment, or supply invoices.

Student Activity

After checking the report, say this out loud: “An aging report shows outstanding balances, not appointments, full clinical notes, or all completed payments.” Then answer the practice questions.

A/R and A/P

Students often confuse these because the abbreviations look similar. Keep the direction of the money clear.

A/RMoney owed to the provider
A/PMoney owed by the practice

Accounts Receivable

Money owed to the provider. This includes unpaid insurance claims and patient balances for services already rendered.

Memory cue: the practice should receive this money.

Accounts Payable

Money owed by the practice. This includes rent, vendor invoices, equipment bills, supply charges, and office services.

Memory cue: the practice must pay this money.

Learning Outcome

By the end of this page, you should be able to sort examples into A/R or A/P and explain your choice using one sentence.

Formulas and Calculations

Some CBCS questions ask you to calculate a remaining balance, total A/R, or proper aging bucket. Keep the formulas simple and repeat them until they feel automatic.

Chargetotal billed amount
− Paymentamount paid
− Adjustmentamount adjusted off
= Balancestill owed

Balance Formula

Use this when you are given charge, payment, and adjustment.

Remaining balance = Charge − Payment − Adjustment

Example: 700 − 300 − 100 = 300

Total A/R Formula

Use this when you add all outstanding balances owed to the practice.

Total A/R = Sum of all unpaid payer and patient balances

Example: 900 + 350 + 250 = 1,500

Aging Bucket Logic

Use days outstanding to place the claim in the correct range.

0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–120, 120+

Example: 50 days = 31–60

Worked Example

Question: A claim shows $640 charged, $190 paid, and $50 adjusted. What is the remaining balance?

Work: 640 − 190 − 50 = 400

Answer: The remaining balance is $400.

Student Activity

  • Say the balance formula without looking.
  • Add the unpaid balances in the examples to practice total A/R.
  • Place 22 days, 48 days, 84 days, and 130 days in the correct buckets.

Mini Scenario 1

A claim to UHC is 83 days old. The charge was $650, $200 was paid, and $450 remains. Before billing the patient, what should you review first?

Best thinking: Review the EOB first so you know whether the remaining amount is patient responsibility, a denial, a contractual adjustment, or a secondary payer balance.

Mini Scenario 2

A denial worklist shows “member ID error” for a BCBS claim. What is the most reasonable next step?

Best thinking: Verify the member ID, correct the claim data, and resubmit the claim.

Write a Short Follow-Up Note

Prompt: You called the payer about a 129-day-old claim. The representative said the claim was denied because the patient was not eligible on the date of service. Write a short follow-up note describing what happened and what should happen next.
